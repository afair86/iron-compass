A weekly review is a short appointment with your responsibilities. Look at what happened, decide what to do with unfinished commitments and make a realistic plan for the next seven days.

Start with twenty minutes if that is available. The suggested sequence gives four minutes to gathering loose ends, four to reviewing evidence, five to choosing priorities, five to making room and two to closing the review. That adds to twenty minutes. The timings are a guide, not another deadline.

Gather tasks, appointments and promises in one place. Check work, household duties and people waiting on you. Resist spending the whole review making the list look organised.

Then ask what you finished, what changed and what you avoided. Distinguish a missed commitment from one that was unrealistic in the first place. Weekly activities belong against the target you set. Three sessions in a week are not seven daily boxes.

Choose up to three outcomes for the next week, each with a clear finish. Choose fewer when caring duties or work leave less room. Include duties already owed before adding a personal project.

Now check fixed appointments, travel, family arrangements and rest. Place the priorities in the time that remains and leave space for disruption. Shared arrangements need agreement with the people involved. Their time is not yours to allocate.

Close by writing the first next action, who needs an update and when you will review again. An unfinished task needs a decision: finish it, reschedule it deliberately, delegate by agreement or remove it. Carrying it forward indefinitely is not a decision.

The article has a free text checklist with the same sequence. Save it, print it or copy the prompts into a notebook. There is no email or account requirement.

If you only have five minutes, check what cannot be missed in the next seven days. Choose one essential outcome, contact one person affected by a change and write the next action. You can preserve a useful review without pretending you have time for a long one.

There is also an optional ninety-minute reset for a week that needs more organising. Fifteen minutes gathers the loose ends. Another fifteen reviews the evidence. Twenty minutes chooses outcomes. Twenty checks the calendar. The last twenty prepares triggers and fallbacks. Fifteen, fifteen, twenty, twenty and twenty makes ninety.

The daily follow-through can be shorter than the weekly review. Know the first action in the morning. At night, note done, partial or missed, write a reason and prepare what comes next. Record the truth rather than protecting a score.

When a demanding week arrives, reduce the plan proportionately. Keep the essentials for work, ordinary health needs and the people who depend on you. If you are too fatigued to train safely, rest or reschedule. Do not count a different activity as the session you agreed to complete.

Over time, check whether fewer commitments are being dropped and whether the calendar fits reality. Those are questions to examine, not guaranteed results. At twelve weeks, keep what you actually use and simplify what you repeatedly avoid.

If your job changes every day, use clear outcomes and leave a buffer instead of pretending every hour is predictable. If another person shares household duties, plan your own priorities first and then agree the shared arrangements.

The review is not an automatic calendar connection or a promise that uncertainty disappears. It is a written set of decisions you can return to. Begin with what must be carried this week, give it realistic space and write the first action.
