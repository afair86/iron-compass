People know you will sort it out. You cover the shift, make the call, take your parent to the appointment and remember which child needs collecting.

Usually, you do sort it out. That is why the extra thing keeps coming to you.

Then a week arrives when the hours no longer fit. You keep saying yes because nobody else seems to have room either. By Thursday you are late, distracted or sharp with someone who has no idea how much you have taken on.

Asking for help works better before that point. You do not need to prove you are at breaking point to make a reasonable request. You need to say what has become too much, which part another person could take, and when you need an answer.

By the end, you will have one clear request, the information needed for a handover, and a fallback if the person cannot help. The planner below turns your answers into wording you can adapt, print or download.

Start by looking at what you have actually agreed to carry.

There is a difference between a demanding week and a week you cannot deliver.

Look at what you have actually agreed to. Include the work other people do not see: organising transport, following up a bill, remembering medication appointments, checking that somebody else got home.

Then ask:

Which two commitments now need the same hours?
What am I repeatedly moving to make the week fit?
Who is relying on an answer I have not given?
What happens if I keep pretending the current plan works?

You might find that one job needs sharing. You might find that something needs to stop. Help is not always another pair of hands. Sometimes the useful conversation is about reducing the work.

If there is no room left, When the Week Is Already Heavy is a related article if you need it. Do not fill the space you recover with a new obligation.

Then make the help specific.

“I need more support” may be an honest beginning. It still leaves the other person guessing what to do.

Try naming a task, a time and the responsibility you want them to take.

“Could you take Mum to her Thursday appointment and handle the return trip? I need to know by Tuesday so I can arrange something else if you cannot.”

That request has edges. The other person can check their own week and answer it. You can plan around the answer.

Be honest about the kind of help you want:

A task taken over: “Can you organise the booking and let me know when it is confirmed?”
An extra pair of hands: “Could you help me move the table on Saturday morning? I’ll have it ready.”
Someone to think with: “Could we spend fifteen minutes deciding which deadline can move?”
Someone to listen: “I don’t need a solution tonight. Have you got ten minutes to hear what is going on?”

Those are different requests. Naming the right one saves both of you a frustrating conversation.

Think about who you are asking, too.

Ask a person who has the ability, the information and a reasonable chance of helping. Being close to you does not make someone automatically available.

At home, check the work your partner is already carrying before handing over more. If the responsibility belongs to both of you, discuss a fair division of ownership rather than treating their contribution as a favour to you.

“We are both stretched. Could we look at pickups and dinner together? I can own Monday and Wednesday. Which parts can you realistically own?”

At work, a colleague might help with a task, while a manager may need to decide priorities. Do not quietly pass your impossible deadline to somebody with less power to refuse it.

A child should not become the person who manages your adult worries. Age-appropriate jobs are one thing; making them responsible for keeping you emotionally steady is another.

Here is what an early request might sound like.

There is no perfect script. These examples show the shape; adapt the words so they sound like you.

At work: “I have the stocktake and the client report due Friday. I can finish one properly in the time available. Which takes priority, and can we move the other or share it?”

With a sibling: “I’ve covered the last three appointments. Can you organise the next one, including transport? If not, let’s decide another arrangement this weekend.”

With a mate: “The move is bigger than I expected. Are you free for an hour Saturday to help with the heavy furniture? Fine if you cannot; I need to organise the help by Thursday.”

You do not need a long defence of why you deserve assistance. Give enough context for a useful answer. Ask once, clearly, and let the person check.

If this involves changing an existing promise, say that too. Keep Your Word covers the early update you owe the person who was counting on you.

Once someone agrees, there is another part to practise: letting them help.

Accepting help can be harder than asking for it.

You ask someone to organise dinner, then send six messages about brands, timing and the way you normally do it. The task has moved, but you are still trying to run every part of it.

Agree on what matters: the result, any real constraints and when to check back. If allergies, access needs or a firm closing time matter, say so. If the order of the shopping list is only your preference, leave it alone.

“Dinner for four, one person cannot have nuts, and we need to eat before the appointment. The rest is yours.”

Make sure the information and access are there. A handover is not complete if somebody has to chase you for the address, booking number or permission to make a decision.

Thank them plainly afterwards. Resist turning help into an immediate debt you must repay with a bigger favour.

Of course, the answer may be no.

A clear request does not guarantee a yes. The other person may have limits you cannot see.

Accept the answer. Then revisit the plan: ask someone else, reduce the job, change the date or tell the person waiting what cannot be done. Do not leave the original promise silently in place.

If a shared responsibility repeatedly returns to you, that needs a separate conversation about ownership. Repeating a smaller request every week may leave the unfair arrangement untouched. Choose a calm time and name the pattern with specific examples.

Here is an example of how a week could work.

Assume a man has two fixed workdays on site, his father's appointment on Thursday and school pickup that afternoon. His sister lives nearby, but he has never asked whether she can cover an appointment.

On Monday he asks her to own the Thursday trip, including checking the appointment time. She can take their father there but cannot bring him home. They agree she will handle the outward trip and he will arrange the return with another willing adult. He confirms both journeys before changing his work plans.

At home, he and his partner check pickups against both calendars. They discover neither can cover Thursday. They ask an established, trusted carer who is already authorised to collect the child, and wait for confirmation.

The help is real only when the arrangements are agreed. Sending two messages and hoping is not the same as a covered afternoon.

If nobody is available, he goes back to work early in the week to change the commitment. He does not ask his family to absorb an arrangement he has already promised elsewhere.
