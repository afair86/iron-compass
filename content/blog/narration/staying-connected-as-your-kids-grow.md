There was a time when they brought you everything. A drawing. A loose tooth. A question before you had finished answering the last one.

Now the bedroom door is closed. Their friends know the names you are still learning. You ask about school and get “fine”. You still do the driving, the cooking, the paying and the worrying, but some evenings you wonder whether you are actually close.

Fatherhood changes as your children grow. The old ways of spending time together may stop fitting. You can miss those days without asking your child to stay the person they were in them.

The work now is to learn who is in front of you, keep making room for them and become someone they can talk to without every conversation turning into a correction.

This guide will help you choose a small opening that suits your child's stage, make an invitation and keep one promise. The planner below produces a weekly plan you can print or download, without scoring your child or requiring an account.

Begin by letting the relationship change shape.

Wanting more time with friends or more privacy does not, by itself, mean your child is rejecting you.

Raising Children Network’s guide to staying connected with teenagers describes both everyday moments and planned time together as ways to maintain closeness while young people become more independent.

Use that as a starting point. You do not need to recreate the Saturday mornings they loved at six. Ask what they enjoy now.

“We haven’t done much together lately. What would you actually like to do?”

They might want to show you a game, make food, go somewhere with you or simply have a lift without a lecture. Some invitations will fall flat. Stay interested without demanding enthusiasm on cue.

Then pay attention to who your child is now.

You may know their favourite meal from three years ago and not know which friend they sit with at lunch today.

Begin with curiosity about something already in their life. Ask them to show you how it works. Remember one detail and return to it later.

“You said the audition was today. How did it feel?”

“Is that the person you were building the project with?”

You do not need to share every interest. You do need to avoid making them defend it before they have finished telling you about it.

With younger children, join a few minutes of what they are doing and let them explain. With an older child, ask whether they would like company rather than assuming you can enter every activity. With an adult child, check what involvement is welcome and offer advice with permission.

Those are possibilities, not age rules. Fit the invitation to your child's interests, communication needs and temperament. Talking face to face is one option; doing something alongside each other, exchanging a message or sitting quietly can also make room for contact.

You can change the question as they grow, too.

The question that worked last year may have become too broad, too personal or simply boring. Try one opening tied to something they have already shown you.

With a younger child: “Can you show me how you made that?” Join the activity for a while. Follow their explanation without turning it into a quiz or improving the thing they were pleased to show you.

With a teenager: “You mentioned that was happening today. Do you feel like talking about it?” If they say no, accept it and stay available. A lift or a meal together can remain pleasant without a full report of their day.

With an adult child: “Would you like another view, or are you still thinking it through?” You can care about the decision without assuming you should make it. Let them decide how much detail to share.

These are suggested openings, not scripts that guarantee a conversation. Use language and a way of communicating that fit your child.

Keep some of your time together free of evaluation.

Homework matters. Manners matter. You still need to ask about the things you are responsible for.

But listen to the balance of your conversations. If nearly every exchange is about what they have not done, time with you may start to feel like an inspection.

Keep an ordinary moment in which the point is being together. Making lunch. Walking to the shop. The drive after training. A game they choose.

Let them be funny, distracted or interested in something you would never choose yourself. You can enjoy their company without turning the moment into a lesson.

For younger school-age children, Raising Children Network suggests shared activities and following their interests, as well as allowing space before asking lots of questions after school.

Your child does not have to earn your interest with a good result.

When they do talk, listen long enough to understand what they need.

A child starts telling you about an argument. You can already see what they should have done. You interrupt with the answer, and the story stops.

Try waiting.

Ask one question that helps you understand: “What happened after that?” or “Which part is bothering you most?”

Then check whether they want help: “Would you like me to think it through with you, or do you want me to listen first?” With a younger child, simpler words may fit: “Tell me. I’m listening.”

Listening does not mean agreeing with every choice. You can hear why something mattered to them and still deal with behaviour that needs attention.

If they tell you something difficult, start steadily. “Thank you for telling me. Let’s work out what needs to happen.” Do not promise secrecy you cannot keep if someone is unsafe. Explain who needs to help and involve your child in the next step where possible.

You are making it possible to talk honestly, including on days when the answer will involve a limit or a consequence.

A small ritual can give you a place to start.

A regular point of contact can be useful because neither of you has to keep inventing it.

Breakfast together before Saturday sport. Preparing one meal. A short walk with the dog. A call when you work away, at a time that suits the child as well as you.

Agree the shape together. Let them choose sometimes. If the old activity has become a chore, change it rather than accusing them of not caring.

If you have more than one child, make some room to know each one separately. The child who asks loudly is easier to notice; the quieter one still deserves an invitation. The time does not have to be identical, and you do not need to keep a scoreboard.

Ten minutes is an example of a manageable start, not a proven dose of fatherhood. Choose time you can give attentively and repeat when it suits you both.

If you live or work apart, dependability still matters.

If your children move between homes, or your work takes you away, make the contact you agree to reliable. Check arrangements with the other responsible adult where needed and keep them appropriate to the child's routine and any existing contact arrangements.

Do not ask your child to carry messages between adults, report on the other household or reassure you that you are the preferred parent.

You might agree on a short call, send a photo of something they would enjoy or remember to ask how the school event went. Let them have ordinary things to say; every call does not need a heartfelt update.

If you will miss an agreed call, communicate through the appropriate channel early and offer a realistic alternative. Avoid promising a big outing to make up for an absence before you know you can deliver it.

There will also be moments to repair.

Perhaps you laughed at something they were serious about. You checked your phone while they were talking. You arrived angry and turned a small mistake into a hard evening.

Name your part without asking them to comfort you.

“I interrupted you and made a joke when you were trying to tell me something. I’m sorry. If you want to tell me again, I’ll listen.”

Or: “The rule still stands. The way I spoke to you was wrong.”

An apology does not require an immediate hug, forgiveness or another attempt at the conversation. Give them room. Then let your behaviour make the apology believable.

Here are three illustrations of how connection can change.

These are examples, not expectations for every child or household.

A primary-school child. Assume a father is home for dinner twice during the working week. His eight-year-old no longer wants to recount school at the door. On one evening they make wraps together. He lets the child choose the fillings and hears about a playground game while they prepare them. He asks about that game later in the week, without testing whether the story matches.

A teenager. Assume a fifteen-year-old is willing to spend a short time with her father, but dislikes planned serious talks. She chooses music while they wash up. He asks about one artist and listens without complaining about the song. When she mentions a disagreement with a friend, he asks whether she wants advice before offering it. The dishes get done; the conversation is allowed to end naturally.

An adult child living away. Assume both want more contact but their working hours differ. They agree to try a short call on an evening that suits both. He asks about the new job and shares something from his week. When the conversation turns to a decision, he asks, “Would another opinion help?” He accepts the answer, including when it is no.

Different stages, different invitations. In each, the father is paying attention to the person his child is now.

Some weeks time is scarce. Sometimes the contact itself is difficult.

On a full week, join something already happening rather than organising an event. Put the phone away for part of the meal. Listen to the story they start. Ask about the thing they said mattered yesterday.

If they decline an invitation, stay warm and leave another opening later. Do not make them responsible for your disappointment.

This article cannot settle estrangement, unsafe family situations or contact disputes. Where those are involved, respect safety and contact arrangements and seek suitable professional support. A routine invitation is not a substitute for addressing the reason contact is difficult.

Think about one child at a time. The planner asks about their current interests, an invitation that could fit and your own promise. It adapts the guidance for a younger child, teenager or adult child, and for ordinary time together, living apart or a repair you need to make.

Use a role such as “my youngest” in your own notes, and leave out names, school details or private disclosures. The result is a plan for your behaviour. It does not assess your child, diagnose the relationship, send an invitation or make contact arrangements.

If you do not know what matters to them at the moment, begin by asking rather than guessing an answer for the form. After the time together, review whether you listened and kept your promise. Do not judge the result by how grateful or talkative your child was.

Your children will keep changing. Let your attention change with them. Make one invitation that suits who they are now, and be there for the time you agree.
