You see something he would find funny. You nearly send it. Then you remember you never replied to his last message, and now it has been four months.

You tell yourself you will call when there is time for a proper catch-up.

Another month goes.

Some friendships end because something happened. Others become quiet because the ordinary places you used to meet disappear. Different jobs. Children. A move. A roster that stopped lining up with his.

If this is a good friendship you have simply let drift, you can get in touch without accounting for every missing week. Send an honest message and offer something small enough that either of you could actually do it.

Leave with a first message you can use, a realistic invitation and a plan for what to do if the timing does not suit or there is no reply. The message planner below works without an account and does not contact anyone.

First, be honest about why you lost touch.

Before you reach out, ask whether you lost contact or left something unresolved.

If the last exchange was friendly and life got full, a simple hello is enough. If you cancelled twice, borrowed something you never returned or said something hurtful, deal with your part of that directly.

“I went quiet after cancelling on you. That was poor follow-through from me. I’m sorry. I’d like to catch up if you’re open to it.”

Do not hide an apology inside an invitation and expect the coffee to clear the debt. Equally, do not invent a serious falling-out because two busy people stopped messaging.

This piece is for friendships you both have reason to welcome. You do not owe renewed access to somebody who repeatedly mistreats you. If a person has asked for no contact, respect that.

When you reach out, make it easy to answer.

A message that says “We should catch up sometime” leaves all the work for later.

A better one gives him a reason you thought of him and an easy next step.

“Drove past the old workshop today and thought of you. Been too long. Fancy a walk Sunday morning? I’m free from eight to nine.”

You can be warmer or less formal. The useful parts are the same: a real hello, a specific possibility and room to say it does not suit.

Three starting points:

After ordinary drift: “Been a while. How have you been? I’ve got half an hour Thursday evening if you fancy a call.”
When life has changed: “Heard you’ve moved. How are you finding it? Would be good to hear how things are going when you have a gap.”
When you have little capacity yourself: “Saw this and thought you’d appreciate it. No big update from me; just wanted to say hello.”

That last message is still contact. Every exchange does not need to become a scheduled event.

Keep the first catch-up manageable.

You might be imagining a whole Saturday, a late dinner or the trip you have been discussing for years. Those may happen later. Start with the time you have.

Coffee before a shift. A short walk. A call after both households have settled. Kicking a ball while the children play, if that suits everyone involved.

Check the real cost of the plan. A forty-minute catch-up with an hour of driving is a different request from a forty-minute catch-up nearby. If a partner or another carer needs to cover your absence, agree that rather than assuming it.

Give one or two options. Do not create a group chat and twenty possible dates when you want to see one person.

If you live apart, a short call can do the job. Ask when he is free to talk; do not assume his commute is safe or private enough for the conversation.

And let the first meeting be ordinary.

You do not need to recover six months in the first ten minutes.

Ask about something you remember. His new role. The house repairs. How his daughter is finding school. Then listen to the answer rather than waiting to deliver your own update.

If you cannot remember where things stood, say so: “I’ve lost track of what happened with that job. How did it turn out?”

Leave room for enjoyment. You can talk about the match, the terrible coffee or something ridiculous you both remember. A good catch-up does not have to produce a serious disclosure to count.

If the conversation does become serious, stay with what he is saying. “Do you want me to listen, or would it help to think through options?” is often more useful than an immediate plan for fixing his life.

Share something honest yourself, too. Asking questions for half an hour while revealing nothing can make a friend feel interviewed.

What if he cannot make it, or there is no reply?

“Cannot do Sunday” is an answer about Sunday. It may not be an answer about the friendship.

If he offers another time, work with it. If he sounds interested but cannot plan, leave a simple opening: “No worries. Let me know when your roster settles.”

With no reply, one gentle follow-up after a reasonable gap may be appropriate in a friendship where that would be welcome. For example, after a week or two: “Just checking this didn’t get buried. Would be good to see you when things ease up.” That is a practical suggestion, not a rule about how quickly friends must respond.

Then leave the choice with him. Do not switch across several apps, involve other people to chase him or turn silence into an argument.

You can make a clear invitation. You cannot make somebody resume a friendship at the pace you want.

If you do catch up, make the next contact a little easier.

If you both enjoyed seeing each other, say so before leaving. Ask whether you would like to do it again.

“That was good. Want to try another walk next month, once we know our weekends?”

Keep any arrangement small enough to survive a busy life. Some mates will enjoy a regular breakfast. Others will do better with the occasional call and a shared interest that keeps the conversation open. Agree something between you rather than assigning him a friendship routine.

If you promise to send a date, send it. If circumstances change, speak early. Keep Your Word applies to the casual promises as much as the work ones.

Here is an example of how the next fortnight could work.

Assume two friends live fifteen minutes apart. One works alternating weekends; the other has Saturday morning sport with his child. Neither can leave a whole weekend free.

On Tuesday, one sends a message offering a Sunday walk. His mate is working but suggests the following Wednesday after dinner. They agree on a twenty-minute call instead because travel would use most of the available time.

The call happens. They talk about work, a mutual friend and a small problem one has been putting off. Nobody gets through every update.

Before finishing, they agree to look for a walk on the next weekend they both have free. One checks the roster and sends two options when it arrives. They confirm one.

The first useful result is a conversation they enjoyed. The next is a plan both have actually agreed to. There is no need to turn either into a measure of how good a mate you are.

Some weeks there will be very little room.

Send the thing that reminded you of him. Add one honest line.

“Flat out this week, but this made me think of you. How’s the new place?”

Do not offer a call you already know you cannot make. Small contact with honest limits is easier to sustain than a generous invitation you keep cancelling.
