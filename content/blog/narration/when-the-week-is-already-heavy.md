Some weeks do not need a better attitude. They need you to stop adding things.

A deadline moves forward. A child is off colour. A parent needs a call. The money is tighter than you thought on Sunday. By Wednesday you are either grinding until you are short with everyone, or you drop the lot and call it a write-off.

There is a middle. You stay decent, you sleep, you eat, and you keep the one promise that other people are already counting on. The rest can wait.

Before you reorganise your life, write one line. What is the load this week? Work that will not fit. A person who needs you. Money. Your own body. If you cannot name it, every irritation feels the same size.

On a heavy week, do not invent a new routine. Keep three things.

Sleep. Most adults need about seven to nine hours. That is general guidance, not a personal prescription. Being in bed is not the same as being asleep. Pick a lights-out time you can actually keep, and protect it three nights this week. If you are in bed at half past ten and the alarm is six, that is seven and a half hours available. You may not sleep all of it. You have still given the night a chance.

One proper meal. Not a perfect diet. One meal you sit down for. The other meals can be plain.

And the promise you already made. Pickup. The call. The draft you said you would send. If you can only keep one agreement this week, keep the one another person has planned around.

Training, if it happens, stays small. Do not add it on a night when you are too tired to drive or to trust your balance.

Illness, a night shift, or a real crisis can remove even those three things. Say so, in one sentence, to the person who needs to know. Sleep when you safely can. Eat something real once. Tell the person waiting what you can still do. If the heaviness is a low mood that will not lift, or thoughts of harming yourself, this is the wrong help. In Australia, Lifeline is 13 11 14, at any hour.

Picture a week. He has pickup on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday, and a report that grew. He does not add an early session to prove he can cope. He leaves on time for those three pickups. He stays back one hour on two other days, not three. Lights out is half past ten on three nights. One night goes late because a child is unwell. He does not punish the next day. He sends a short note: the report will be Monday, and here is what is already done.

Do not start a new program because you feel behind. Do not skip sleep to look committed. Do not make the household carry a mood you have not named. One sentence is enough. This week is heavy. I am still doing pickup.

Name the load in one line. Protect sleep on the nights you can, eat one proper meal, and keep the promise someone else is counting on. If the week takes even that, say what you can still do.
