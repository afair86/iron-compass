Some questions land in private, late at night:

Am I doing enough?  
Can the people I love rely on me?  
Do my actions match the man I think I am?

Those questions are a starting point. They are not a verdict.

Judging your entire identity in one swing usually produces either harsh self-attack or cheap reassurance. Neither helps the people around you.

What helps is examining **specific actions**, noticing impact, and choosing one behaviour to practise.

If you are looking for easy comfort — “If you’re asking, you’re already a good man” — this is not that article.

## Actions are not your whole worth

You can take a hard look at your behaviour without deciding you are worthless.

You can also refuse to use “accountability” as a reason to accept all blame, abandon boundaries, or tolerate mistreatment.

The aim is clearer seeing and steadier follow-through — not a personality label.

## Optional roles to examine

Use only the roles that fit your life. Skip the rest.

### As a man

- Do my choices match my values when nobody is watching?  
- Can I admit mistakes without immediately defending myself?  
- Do I take responsibility for what I can change?

### As a partner or husband

- Do I listen to understand, or prepare my defence?  
- Do I share responsibility for noticing, planning, and doing household tasks?  
- Can my partner raise concerns without being mocked, dismissed, or intimidated?  
- Do I show affection, appreciation, and interest?  
- Do I respect boundaries, independence, and consent?  
- Do I communicate my own needs respectfully?  
- Does my behaviour change after an apology?

Accountability here does not mean absorbing unfair attacks. It means you can hear a real concern without turning the moment into a contest.

### As a father or caregiver

- Do I listen, show interest, and follow through?  
- Can my children bring me difficult news?  
- Do I apologise and change when I get something wrong?  
- Are my expectations appropriate to their age and circumstances?

Do not ask children to judge your worth or manage your adult emotions. That is your work.

### As a son

- What do care and respect look like in this relationship?  
- What boundaries are necessary?  
- Am I acting from my values rather than guilt alone?

Bereavement, estrangement, and unsafe relationships are real. Being a good son does **not** require obedience, contact, or reconciliation with someone who harms you. Grief and complicated family history deserve care; if you need support, speak with a qualified professional or a trusted service in your area.

### As a leader

Leadership can be at work, at home, or in the community.

- Can people disagree with me?  
- Do I take responsibility and share credit?  
- Are expectations clear?  
- Do I respond fairly to mistakes?  
- Do I practise the standards I ask of others?

For a related integrity lens at work, see [The Integrity Audit](/blog/integrity-audit-leadership).

## A reflection process you can reuse

1. Choose a value.  
2. Describe a recent situation.  
3. Identify what you actually said or did.  
4. Consider the impact — separate facts from assumptions.  
5. Recognise what worked.  
6. Own what needs attention.  
7. Choose one action and review it later.

## Brief worked examples (illustrative)

**Partner — household workload.**  
Value: fairness. Situation: your partner says the mental load of school admin is falling on them. You said, “I work longer hours,” and changed the subject. Impact (fact): the admin still sat with them; they felt unheard. What worked: you stayed in the room. What needs attention: listening without defence. Next action: ask what two tasks you can own this week, and do the first one tomorrow. Review: Sunday.

**Child — distraction.**  
Value: presence. Situation: your child tried to show you something while you scrolled. You muttered “in a minute” and forgot. Impact: they stopped asking. Next action: phone in another room for the first twenty minutes after you get home for three evenings. Review: Friday.

**Parent — defensiveness.**  
Value: respect. Situation: a parent criticised your choices; you snapped and listed their failures. Even if the criticism was unfair, the escalation was yours. Next action: if you continue the conversation, name one boundary and one calm sentence — or pause contact for now if the relationship is unsafe. Review: in one week, privately, without demanding a child mediate.

**Colleague — displaced frustration.**  
Value: fairness. Situation: after a rough morning you spoke sharply to a junior colleague about a small mistake. Next action: apologise specifically, correct the tone in front of others if needed, and ask what they need to finish the task. An apology does not entitle you to instant forgiveness.

## Asking for feedback

A useful question:

“What is one thing I do that helps, and one thing I could do differently?”

Ask someone who has seen you in the role. Listen without cross-examining. You can still disagree later. First, receive it.

## Repair and follow-through

Repair is changed behaviour over time.

An apology without change teaches people that your words are theatre. Change without acknowledgement can still leave a wound. Aim for both when you have caused harm — and accept that forgiveness is theirs to give or withhold.
